How the traffic is built

Each forest hex (842 km²) sends its FIA removals to every mill within the haul cap, weighted by travel time: a mill as many minutes farther than the nearest as the spread setting receives about 37% of the nearest mill’s share. Spread zero means the nearest mill only, the original model. Road thickness is the wood summed over every route crossing a segment; each haul counts once, and the empty return uses the same road.

What “forest” means here

FIA forest land is land at least 10% stocked with trees, at least 1 acre and 120 ft wide; it excludes urban and farm trees. FIA reports county totals, so every hex takes its county’s share, and where dots fall inside a hex is illustrative. Each dot is about 19 km² of forest. Hexes under 20% forest are drawn but not treated as wood sources.

The grey-green underlay is the USFS NLCD tree canopy cover (percent of each 30 m pixel under tree crowns, v2025-6), averaged to 2 km, for 2021. It is a satellite-modelled measure and is not the FIA definition of forest: it includes street and yard trees and can miss open woodland. FIA gives only county totals, so you can place each county’s removals evenly across its hexes or in proportion to canopy; county totals are the same either way.

Limits of the data

Mills are a 117-mill sample with no recorded closure, so empty areas (Texas, Oklahoma, Kentucky) show missing mills as much as distance. Roads are Natural Earth 1:10m major roads; speeds of 48–80 km/h and 1.10 circuity are assumed. Mill capacity and species are ignored, and there are no truck-route or weight limits.

Data: USDA Forest Service FIA county estimates (annual removals and forest area, 2016–2025 evaluations); Natural Earth 1:10m roads; US Census state and county boundaries via us-atlas; author-compiled mill list (Forisk / PPRC 2025, company announcements, trade press). Map: spherical Albers equal-area conic, standard parallels 29.5°N and 45.5°N, origin 37.5°N 96°W.
